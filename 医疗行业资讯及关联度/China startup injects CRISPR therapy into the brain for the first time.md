China startup injects CRISPR therapy into the brain for the first time
Ryan Cross Senior Science Correspondent
Late last year, doctors injected an [[experimental]] CRISPR therapy into the brain of a 9-year-old boy with a rare neurodevelopmental condition that can cause intellectual disability, movement problems and seizures.

Before the treatment, the boy struggled to walk without somebody holding both of his hands, according to a video recording. He didn’t respond to verbal directions to toss a ball or to stack toy blocks.

But four weeks later, he could hand off a ball. After 12 weeks, he could walk more smoothly and was able to more deftly stack the blocks.

The results of the genetic disease — an otherwise fatal disorder called MECP2 duplication syndrome — are preliminary. But the study is remarkable for two reasons. It’s the first to test a CRISPR-based treatment in the brain. And like an increasing number of firsts in gene editing, it was run by a relatively little-known, modestly funded Chinese biotech, this one called HuidaGene Therapeutics.

At a medical conference in Copenhagen this month where the results were shared, HuidaGene also unveiled preliminary results from two boys who were treated with its experimental CRISPR therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy. Although multiple US drugmakers have announced intentions to develop similar gene editing therapies for Duchenne, those latest American-led efforts appear nowhere close to a clinical trial.


Alvin Luk
The Shanghai-based startup’s latest achievements come amid mounting concerns that American science is losing its edge, blunted by cuts to federal research grants and investors favoring less risky ideas. But under the leadership of Alvin Luk — a gene therapy veteran with stints at Bayer, Biogen and Spark Therapeutics — HuidaGene has been steadily planting flags in brain, eye and muscle diseases.

The company has granted few interviews until now, but Luk spoke to Endpoints News last week about its first clinical trials for its MECP2 and muscular dystrophy therapies, as well as the company’s business strategy, focused on outlicensing or partnering with bigger drugmakers.

Luk told Endpoints that when he first joined HuidaGene in 2023, he was shocked by how little they had shared publicly about their scientific progress. “Nobody has heard about you guys,” he recalls telling the company’s scientists. They submitted a backlog of research to conferences that year and have tried to “continue to maintain this momentum” ever since, he said.

Marco Weinberg, head of research at the California-based biotech ReCode Therapeutics, was one of the few US scientists speaking at the Copenhagen meeting, and sat in on HuidaGene’s presentation.

“It has been hard for Chinese companies to gain the same respect and attention as their US counterparts — maybe that will change,” Weinberg told Endpoints in an email. HuidaGene’s results show “how competitive China has become in the space dominated in the past by US biotech, and this will only continue to grow.”

An overlooked leader in gene editing


Marco Weinberg
HuidaGene was founded in 2018 based on research by Hui Yang, a professor at the Chinese Academy of Sciences Institute of Neuroscience in Shanghai, who uses CRISPR to develop gene-edited monkeys for disease modeling, and has developed several gene editing tools.

Yang cut his teeth on the technology as a post-doc at MIT for two years before returning to China in 2014 as part of the country’s Thousand Talents Plan, which seeks to draw back Chinese scientists studying and working abroad. Most of HuidaGene’s 60 employees are based in Shanghai.

The startup has attracted scant attention in the US, possibly because it has said little publicly about its funding and investors. That may change after the data it presented in Copenhagen.

HuidaGene gave a low dose of its therapy to two boys with MECP2 duplication. After three months, the first patient, a 9-year-old boy with an IQ of an 11-month-old, appeared to have improved eye contact, motor skills, walking ability and was able to respond to simple directions such as “come to eat” or “go to the bathroom.”

The second patient had only been followed for one month after treatment, and the company did not share updates on the patient’s progress. HuidaGene said that there were no severe adverse events related to the therapy, but one of the patients was hospitalized for an unrelated infection.

MECP2 happens when patients have an extra copy of the gene, leading to over-expression. But too little MECP2 expression causes another neurodevelopment condition called Rett syndrome. To strike the right balance, HuidaGene uses a CRISPR enzyme dubbed Cas13Y to tamp down short-lived mRNA molecules of the finicky gene, rather than DNA, hoping to normalize its levels.

It’s only the second time that a CRISPR-based RNA editing therapy has been tested in humans. The first was HuidaGene’s study in a form of macular degeneration in 2023. The FDA recently cleared a Phase 1 study for that program in the US. No American biotech companies appear close to testing the technology in people.

Developing genetic treatments for Duchenne muscular dystrophy poses its own set of challenges, including the massive size of dystrophin, the vital muscle protein that can be broken by a slew of genetic mutations.

HuidaGene’s therapy uses a version of the molecular scissors known as Cas12 to target a mutation in a region of the dystrophin gene known as exon 51. Depending on how the cell’s DNA repair machinery fixes the cut made by CRISPR, the therapy causes cells to either skip over the mutations and produce a shortened but partially functional dystrophin (a similar mechanism as Exondys 51, a chronic treatment sold by Sarepta Therapeutics), or shift the gene’s reading frame to restore the entire muscle protein.

HuidaGene uses AAV to deliver its CRISPR therapy, and getting enough of the medicine to the body’s many muscle cells requires massive doses, often topping hundreds of trillions of viral particles.

At least four patients have died from DMD gene therapies, including two from Pfizer’s experimental treatment and one who received Sarepta’s approved therapy Elevidys, which both deliver shortened versions of the massive dystrophin gene. Another US patient, the first to receive a custom CRISPR therapy for his rare form of Duchenne, also died, likely from an immune reaction to AAV. That death loomed large when trying to find doctors willing to test the therapy.

“We reached out to at least 10 hospitals,” Luk said. Some hadn’t heard of CRISPR, while others had heard about the death and didn’t want anything to do with the technology. The CRISPR baby scandal in China in 2018 also lingered like a “dark cloud” for some doctors, Luk said.

“In the West, we think you can be a cowboy in China, but that’s not true,” he said. (It’s an image likely not helped by the recent viral social media posts of the scientist, Jiankui He, behind the baby scandal.)

HuidaGene has given its Duchenne therapy to two boys, ages 6 and 8, with no severe adverse events that have caused serious safety concerns in other gene therapy programs. The company shared data suggesting subtle improvements three months after the first patient was treated. Most notably, the boy was able to walk 452 meters in the commonly used 6-minute walk test, 55 meters further than before he got the therapy. On another assessment of motor skills, it showed a modest improvement. (Another Chinese biotech, GenAssist Therapeutics, earlier this year produced roughly similar results with its own CRISPR base editing therapy for Duchenne.)

The DMD and MECP2 therapies were both tested in an investigator-initiated trial, where academic or hospital researchers can move forward with less stringent requirements. Luk said the studies help it de-risk the programs and that he plans to take the data to the FDA to get clearance for Phase 1 clinical trials in the US.

Luk said that he is planning on testing higher doses of both therapies in China with the startup’s current funds, but he will need to find another drugmaker willing to pay for future Phase 1 studies.

An expanding pipeline and toolkit

For its brain and muscle disease therapies, HuidaGene is using a common vector called AAV9. Many US companies have been hesitant to use AAVs, which continue producing the CRISPR machinery long after it is needed for therapy. Since CRISPR proteins come from microbes, some worry that long-term expression might cause the immune system to attack edited cells, nullifying the therapy’s benefits and harming the patient.

HuidaGene is now developing a new version of AAV vectors that can deliver short-lived RNA molecules, instead of DNA, letting the CRISPR therapy do its job and get cleared from cells.

“Many of our competitors have reached out to us for this tool,” Luk said.

HuidaGene’s most recent slide deck, reviewed by Endpoints, indicates that it has raised $132 million in four rounds of funding between 2019 and 2022, largely through Chinese investors including Sherpa Healthcare Partners, CD Capital and WuXi AppTec. Luk said HuidaGene is setting out to raise between $27 million and $40 million in a Series C+ financing for a new set of programs at the company, including gene editing treatments for ALS and Alzheimer’s disease, as well as a new approach for DMD that he hopes might work for all forms of the disease, regardless of a patient’s mutation.

While many CRISPR companies in the US are focused on a single gene editing technology, HuidaGene has developed a deep toolkit, including Cas12 enzymes for DNA cutting and Cas13 for RNA knockdown, plus tools for base editing and epigenetic editing.

“We can choose which tool is the best for the disease,” Luk said. It was a major factor in his decision to join the company as CEO in 2023, he said, after consulting for them a year prior.

The company is now considering partnerships that could advance the programs.

“We cannot carry all the way to Phase 3. There’s no way. If there’s no partnership, we cannot do that,” Luk said. “But at least we have human proof-of-concept data.”
